The sale of electric trucks in the Netherlands and across Europe is on the verge of a clear acceleration. According to a recent ING Research report, this shift isn’t happening by chance: it’s driven by policy, technological advancements, and economic pressure. What was once a niche market is rapidly becoming a viable option for transport companies. Here’s why ING believes the e-truck is about to break through.

Diesel is getting more expensive, electric trucks are becoming more attractive

A major turning point is the road freight tax that will be introduced in the Netherlands from July 2026. This tax will make diesel transport roughly 7.5% more expensive. In contrast, electric trucks will benefit from an 80% discount on this same tax. The cost gap widens further in 2027 with the launch of the European Emissions Trading System (ETS 2), which will add about 13 cents per liter of diesel. In total, diesel transport costs are expected to rise by 10 to 12% by 2027, according to ING.

Revenue from the road freight tax will be largely reinvested into subsidies for purchasing electric trucks, reducing their total cost of ownership (TCO) and making the switch more financially viable for fleet operators.

Electric truck fleet set to grow, from 1,500 to 25,000 by 2030

Currently, there are only about 1,500 electric trucks on Dutch roads—less than 1% of the total fleet—but ING expects this number to grow to nearly 25,000 by 2030, representing 15% of the truck fleet.

This growth won’t happen overnight, but will accelerate especially between 2026 and 2030, as regulations, infrastructure, and operational experience begin to align. Supermarkets like AH and Lidl, along with major logistics players such as DSV and Amazon, are leading the way, aiming for fully emission-free logistics by 2030.

Charging infrastructure remains a challenge, but depot charging offers a solution

Limited public charging infrastructure is still a bottleneck. However, the Netherlands, with its short average trip distances (80% <150 km), is actually well-suited for electric trucking. According to ING, around 85% of charging will take place at depots, far above the European average of 60–70%.

Charging on private premises, especially when combined with solar panels, batteries, or dynamic energy contracts, can offer major cost advantages. Still, grid congestion poses a challenge: some businesses won’t get upgraded connections until 2030. Even so, depot charging during mandatory rest breaks is expected to become a logical and efficient solution.

New tech means new ways of operating

One reason adoption remains relatively slow is that electric trucks require changes to operations. Charging takes longer than fueling and demands smart scheduling around driver rest periods. Transport companies also need to gain expertise in energy management—from kWh prices to charging strategies.

But the learning curve is shortening. Electric tractor/trailers already have ranges of 450–600 km, with new models approaching 800 km. More and more routes are becoming not just feasible, but cost-effective for electric trucks.

A wave of fleet replacements is coming, and investments are ramping up

The diesel rush at the end of 2024, as companies raced to buy diesel trucks before zero-emission zones take effect in 2025, will lead to a correction in 2025. But after that, a new investment wave is expected. The current fleet is aging, and with diesel becoming more expensive, electric truck prices falling, and attractive offers from OEMs (such as fleet deals from Volvo and MAN), replacement is becoming increasingly logical.

According to ING, more companies will begin factoring in CO₂ classifications of trucks, which influence kilometer-based road taxes. Smart investors are already planning how to drive each kilometer as efficiently and clean as possible.

The acceleration has begun

Electric trucks are set to become a permanent fixture in Dutch road transport. The landscape is shifting fast: diesel is becoming more expensive, electric is becoming cheaper, and regulations are pushing the sector toward zero-emission operations.

According to ING Research, the tipping point has been reached. In the coming years, transport companies won’t be asking if, but when they’ll go electric.

With the right charging infrastructure, smart planning, and collaboration between stakeholders, the moment is now. The acceleration has started and the road ahead is electric.
